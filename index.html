Take It or Leave It

The new show Chuck built himself. The board holds 36 cases — one for every two-digit number the dice can roll. The first die is the tens spot and the second die is the ones spot, so a 2 and a 1 is 21, and a 6 and a 5 is 65. The cases hold all 36 combinations — 11 through 16, 21 through 26, all the way to 61 through 66 — face down and scrambled into random spots, so nobody knows where a number is hiding. Roll the two dice and they set the number to find: roll a 2 and a 1, and you're hunting for the number 21. Then flip the boxes over one by one until you find it. Try it yourself with the dice right here on the page.

Round by round, cases come off the board and offers land on the table — take the sure thing and walk, or leave it and chase what's still in the cases. The longer they play, the bigger the offers get — and the bigger the risk. It all comes down to one question: take it, or leave it?

Chuck-Tac-Toe — The Signature Game

Chuck's signature game — tic-tac-toe played with dice, and the game the March Madness tournament below is built on. Here are the full rules with the wilds posted right beside them — and you can play a full game right here on the page.

How to Play

  1. Roll off to start. Highest roll goes first and picks X or O.
  2. The board is 3 by 3, with the spaces numbered 1 to 9.
  3. On your turn, roll the first die. Keep that number — or roll the second die and add it to your first roll.
  4. Your total is your space. A total of 1 to 9 lands on that numbered space — and 10, 11 and 12 are the wilds, posted right beside these rules.
  5. Win two ways: get 3 in a row, or be the first to 5 pieces on the board. No cat game — somebody always wins.
  6. Taken is taken. If the number you rolled is already taken, your turn ends with no placement.

Play What's Next Right Here

The computer shuffles one full deck — 52 cards plus 2 jokers — and lays out 12 cards face-down, 3 across by 4 rows. You play against the computer, and you always go first. Play runs bottom row first, left to right, then up through the tiers to the top row. Each turn, Chuck asks: “What's next? Is the next card higher or is it lower?” Guess right and you keep going. Guess wrong and your turn ends and play passes to the other player. Turn up the same rank and you lose your turn too — it is not higher or lower. Aces go both ways: call lower and an Ace counts as a 1, call higher and it counts as an Ace — so an Ace is always a correct guess. Only the last card decides the winner: turn it over with a correct call and you win — miss it, and another card is pulled from the deck to settle it. The replacement lands in a sudden-death row above the top row, and new deciding cards keep coming until somebody turns one over with a correct call — the game never ends on a wrong guess. Pull a joker and you bank it. Jokers can only be used in the top row — spend one to swap a card in the top row for the next card off the deck, which is random and could even be the same rank. And that card can be yours or your opponent's. Use a joker on your own card to get rid of a difficult card like a 7, 8 or 9. Or use it on your opponent's card: if your opponent has an easy card coming up in the top row, spend a joker to swap it for the next card off the deck and make it harder for them. It cuts both ways — if the computer has a joker, it can change your card in the top row the same way.

Hit 4

How to play: the board is 7 columns wide and 6 rows high. Take turns dropping a square into a column — it falls to the lowest open spot. The first player to line up 4 of their squares in a row — across, up and down, or diagonal — wins. Fill the whole board with no line of 4 and it's a draw. Same rules as Connect Four — but here, everything is squares.

Play Hit 4 Right Here

You against the computer — and dice decide who goes first. Before every game there's a roll-off: you and the computer each roll two dice, and the higher total drops first. Tie? You roll again. You play the gold squares and the computer plays the red squares. Tap a red Drop button above a column to drop your square in — then the computer takes its turn. Watch out: it takes a win when it sees one, and it will block you when you're one square away from four.
